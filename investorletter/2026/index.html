Two decisions worth keeping in the record

We would repeat the way we built Anterix. We would not repeat the reason we bought Amtech. The dollars point the other way — Anterix made money and Amtech lost a little — which is why the write-up has to sit next to the result. A gain is not a clean process, and a loss is not automatically a failure of nerve.

We missed the blow-up in memory. We will not buy a stand-in to feel like we caught it.

What Amtech taught us
Repeat · Anterix · +$6,635.63 · +94.96%

The second buy had to be earned.

We bought Anterix in April because of a known catalyst: broader broadband use of the 900 MHz band, and our view that the licenses could support roughly twice the valuation at entry. That was the thesis. It was specific enough to be wrong.

We do not put a full position on at the open. We bought about half, $3,518 on April 17, and added $3,470 on April 21 after the price showed strength and broke the levels we were watching. Eighty-three shares, then seventy-eight. We would take that entry again.

The exit was late. We sold 40 shares in July and the remaining 121 across three sales in September. Net proceeds were $13,623.83 on $6,988.20 of cost. Market conditions had already given us a reason to sell, and we were slow. The rule we are taking from our best contributor is that the trigger to sell has to be as specific as the trigger to add.

Read the transactions we can show

Change · Amtech Systems · −$548.35 · −23.25%

We missed the memory cabal, then reached for a substitute.

Micron, Sandisk, SK Hynix, and Samsung blew up, and a lot of the equipment names around them legged up with them. People call that group the memory cabal for a reason. A handful of companies control the supply, and when the price goes, it goes together. We were not in it. That miss is on us.

The comparison being made is the railroad buildout. The same one that gets made when something real has to be built before anyone can use it. Druckenmiller has used it for years, and he is not the only one using it now. While the track is going in, the company selling the ties looks like it can grow forever. When the track is finished, that demand does not cool off. It falls off. People are making that comparison again, this time about data centers, memory, and the equipment that builds them. The comparison is a good one. It is also an easy way to talk yourself into the wrong stock.

The flow was real. Capital was moving into advanced packaging. Amtech was out talking about business with a hyperscaler, and it looked like a turnaround those flows could carry. We bought it on June 15 for $2,358.63. We were on a different clock than the companies actually taking the orders. Growth slowed. The packaging leaders kept taking share from the smaller names, the same pseudo-monopolies and duopolies that show up across semiconductor equipment. We were out by June 30. Selling was the right call. Buying Amtech so we would not feel left out of the blow-up was not.

Thesis 2 out of 5. Execution 3 out of 5. Sizing is still open. We have not named the hyperscaler, written the price that would have made the idea wrong, or written what we were willing to lose on that $2,358.63.

Amtech Systems −$548.35

Bought June 15 for $2,358.63 · sold June 29–30 · −23.25%

The memory cabal blew up without us. Micron, Sandisk, SK Hynix, and Samsung, and the equipment stocks moving with them. The comparison everyone was using was the railroad buildout, the one Druckenmiller has made often: the tie supplier looks unstoppable while the track is going in, and the demand falls off when the track is done. People are making that same comparison now, about this buildout. We think it is the right comparison to keep. It is a bad reason to buy a substitute.

Capital was moving into advanced packaging. Amtech was touting work with a hyperscaler and looked like a turnaround that could catch it. We bought on June 15 for $2,358.63 and were out June 29 and 30, for a $548.35 loss. Growth slowed. The packaging leaders kept the business. We were early to a theme whose orders were going somewhere else. We would sell it again. We would not buy it to make up for the memory move we missed. Thesis 2, execution 3. Sizing is open. We have not named the hyperscaler, or the loss we had agreed to take.

Regal Rexnord −$880.16

After the SpaceX trade, we thought capital was rotating into robotics, and Regal Rexnord was how we tried to participate. It passed our fundamental screen. We overestimated how fast customers would adopt robots and how fast manufacturers would increase production. We had a view of the destination and not enough work on the timing. For the next trade built on a new product, we want three answers before we buy: who is adopting it today, how quickly supply can meet that demand, and when that should show up in orders or revenue for the company we own.

What changes on the next trade

  1. We still buy about half and add when price confirms the idea. Anterix and Intel are the examples we want to copy. The second buy is a decision, not a habit.
  2. We will not add just because a stock is making new highs if the broader market is going the other way. Oscar is the example we do not want to copy.
  3. When the tape is being compared to the railroad buildout, we own the companies taking the orders, or we own nothing in the theme. We do not buy a smaller name because we missed the move. Amtech is the example, and the memory cabal is the move we missed.
  4. Before a new-product or rotation trade we will write who is adopting it now, how fast supply can respond, and when that shows up in the company’s orders.
  5. The exit trigger gets written down with the same specificity as the add. Anterix’s late sale is the reason.
  6. We sell in pieces, to bank profit without giving up the whole position. After we are out, we look again if the original risk and reward are still intact. Nebius is the case where we did the first and skipped the second.
  7. At entry we will keep a dated note: what we believe, what would prove it wrong, and how much capital we intend to risk. At exit the result goes next to that note, not in place of it. Over the next few letters, you should be able to see whether we followed the rules on this page.
