Two decisions worth keeping in the record

We would repeat the way we built Anterix. We would not repeat the reason we bought Amtech. The dollars run the other way. A gain is not a clean process, and a loss is not a failure of nerve.

The memory stocks ran without us. We will not buy a smaller company to feel as if we caught that move.

What Amtech taught us
Repeat · Anterix · +$6,635.63 · +94.96%

The second buy had to be earned.

Anterix owns licenses in the 900 MHz band. We bought it in April because the FCC was opening that band to broader broadband use, and we thought the licenses could support a price about twice what we paid. That was specific enough to be wrong.

We do not buy the whole position on the first day. We bought 83 shares on April 17 for $3,518, and 78 more on April 21 for $3,470, after the price showed strength and broke the levels we were watching. We would take that entry again.

The exit was late. We sold 40 shares in July and the remaining 121 across three sales in September. Net proceeds were $13,623.83 on $6,988.20 of cost. The market had already given us a reason to sell. The trigger to sell has to be as specific as the trigger to add.

The transactions

Change · Amtech Systems · −$548.35 · −23.25%

We bought the packaging theme through the wrong company.

Micron, Sandisk, SK Hynix, and Samsung ran hard, and a lot of the equipment stocks moved up with them. A few companies supply most of the world’s memory. That is the group people call the memory cabal. When the price goes, it goes together. We did not own it.

Investors are comparing this buildout to the railroads. Druckenmiller has used that comparison for years. While the track is going in, the company selling the ties looks as if it can grow forever. When the track is finished, the orders for ties fall off. The same comparison is being made now about data centers, memory, and the equipment that builds them. The comparison is worth keeping. It is a poor reason to buy a small supplier in place of the companies that are getting the orders.

The money flow was real. Capital was moving into advanced packaging, the step that stacks and connects chips. Amtech was talking about business with a hyperscaler, one of the large cloud companies, and it looked like a turnaround those orders could carry. We bought it on June 15 for $2,358.63. We were early, and we were in the wrong stock. Amtech’s growth slowed. The large packaging companies kept taking business from the smaller ones. In much of semiconductor equipment, two or three companies end up owning the market because they keep the orders. We were out by June 30. The loss was $548.35, or 23.25 percent. The sale was the right call. We would not buy the smaller name to catch a move the leaders had already made.

The idea is a 2 out of 5. The sale is a 3.

Nebius +$2,307.93

169.004 shares sold January 6 and February 5 · cost $11,339.90 · proceeds $13,647.83

We owned Nebius because the most we could lose was defined, and the upside was larger than that downside. We sold it in two pieces, on January 6 and February 5, for a gain of $2,307.93. How we trade

  1. We still buy about half and add when price confirms the idea. Anterix and Intel are the examples we want to copy. The second buy is a decision, not a habit.
  2. We will not add just because a stock is making new highs if the broader market is going the other way. Oscar is the example we do not want to copy.
  3. When investors are comparing a buildout to the railroads, we own the companies getting the orders, or we do not own the theme. We do not buy a smaller company because we missed the move in the leaders. Amtech is the example. The run in memory is the move we missed.
  4. Before a new-product or rotation trade we will write who is adopting it now, how fast supply can respond, and when that shows up in the company’s orders.
  5. The exit trigger gets written down with the same specificity as the add. Anterix’s late sale is the reason.
  6. We sell in pieces, to bank profit without giving up the whole position. After we are out, we look again if the original risk and reward are still intact. Nebius is the case where we did the first and skipped the second.
  7. At entry we write what we believe, what would prove it wrong, and how much capital we intend to risk. The result goes next to that note.
